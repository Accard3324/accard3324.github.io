Here's the scoreboard as I write this: 1,531 commits, 288,921 lines of TypeScript, a test suite that just crossed 5,000, 73 database migrations, 139 routed pages, an iOS app that runs on my phone, and a public changelog with 87 shipping days on it. Paying customers: zero.

That zero is doing more work than any other number in the paragraph, and by the end of this I want you to read it the way I do.

First, who's writing this. I supervise a rental car counter at Hertz in Columbus, Ohio. I have a high school diploma, two years of college I didn't finish, and no formal training in software. I am not technical in the way that word usually gets used. The way I build is: I tell AI agents what I want in plain English, I review what they produce, I test it like a shop owner would use it, and I learn each concept the day it bites me. Four months ago I could not have told you what a database migration was. I'm going to walk through all four months and change, month by month, including what got added, what got deleted, what broke, and what each month forced me to learn, because the learning curve is the actual story. The product is Pitlane, cloud software for independent auto repair shops: repair orders, estimates, text-to-pay, two-way SMS, digital inspections, review automation.

One more piece of context that explains everything else: I built this at night. The peak commit hour across the whole project is 8pm. 260 commits, one in every six, landed between midnight and 6am. I'd close the counter, drive home, and open the laptop.

April: zero to a product in two weeks, then a month of finding out what "done" means

April 10, 2026: initial commit, straight off the Next.js scaffold, under a different name. April 12: renamed to Pitlane, pointed at usepitlane.com. April 13, day three, is a commit literally called "Build full app" that added the API routes, billing, and automations, and the same day added appointments, seasonal campaign templates, win-back sequences, and a sentiment filter on review requests. Hold onto that sentiment filter. It sounds smart, it shipped in an afternoon, and it took me three months to understand why it had to die.

That first week also contains my first real lesson in "the framework is not what the tutorial says." I added a middleware.ts file because that's what every Next.js article tells you to do, discovered it was never executing, and learned this version of Next.js uses a file called proxy.ts instead. Add, remove, same day. There would be a lot of same-day removals.

The rest of April, in honest ledger form: a full redesign to light mode with a command palette. Twilio SMS integration. A four-commit fight with the password reset flow, which is where I learned the acronym PKCE and that authentication cookies do not care about your feelings. An AI campaign wizard. A support ticket system with threads and an admin inbox. Rate limiting, input validation with Zod, security headers. Digital vehicle inspections with per-item video. Estimates, work orders, invoices, reports, inventory, a dispatch board. Stripe Connect so shops collect payments into their own accounts. A recalls feature that shipped April 18 and got removed the same day because it didn't earn its place. A 30-day free trial with feature gating and nurture emails. CAN-SPAM unsubscribe infrastructure. PitCrew, the in-app AI assistant with a morning briefing. Per-visitor sandboxed demo orgs, so every stranger who clicks "demo" gets their own disposable shop. Twenty blog posts. A print booklet called The Follow-Up Playbook, with a cover letter, a scraper that pulled 200 Ohio shops, and hand-addressing reference sheets, because the first marketing plan was me mailing physical booklets. And on April 27, site-wide dark mode, shipped and reverted the same day, then rebuilt properly a day later as an opt-in setting. Ship, learn, undo, redo. That was the whole month in four words.

April 22 was the first security audit, and it's the day I learned what row-level security is: the database itself refuses to hand one shop's rows to another shop, even if my application code screws up. All sixteen tables got it, about forty write routes moved to schema validation, and a SECURITY.md started tracking posture. Same day, the audit found the bug class that would follow me the rest of the project: a PATCH endpoint whose validation coerced missing fields to null, so a partial update silently wiped data the caller never touched. Fixed it in three routes. Told myself I was done. It was not done with me.

Two removals that month that I'm still proud of: the fabricated testimonials came off the homepage a week after they went up, because I decided nothing on the site gets to be fake, and the demo account got hard-blocked from sending real emails or texts to anyone.

What April taught me, in the words I learned that month: migration, RLS, PKCE, webhook, rate limit, CSP. I started the month thinking building software meant writing features. I ended it understanding that every feature is also a door, and doors need locks.

May: the month the product learned to collect money, and I learned what testing is

May opened with a five-wave audit remediation: webhook auth caching, a nonce-based content security policy, user identity aligned with the auth provider, streaming exports, cron retry logic, and a Prisma extension that scopes every database query to the requesting shop as a second line of defense under the RLS. Two days later that same extension broke lookups in a way only surfaced by walking the app, which is how I learned that defense-in-depth also means bugs-in-depth: layers protect you, and layers interact.

Then the real build of May: the repair order became a financial object. A schema for SMS authorization requests, payments, and signatures. Text-to-authorize: a shop texts "can we also replace the brake pads?" with the price, the customer taps approve on their phone, and it's logged with a signature. Text-to-pay: a payment link token, hashed, expiring, opening a branded payment page wired into Stripe Connect, with a receipt emailed after. I learned the difference between bcrypt and SHA-256 the practical way: one is for passwords, and one is for tokens you need to look up by unique index, and I had them backwards on the first pass.

May 7 is a small commit with a big consequence: "add vitest as dev dep." That's the day the project got a unit test runner. Every one of the 5,000 tests traces back to that afternoon. May 13 brought Playwright and the first end-to-end test that walks the whole loop, AI suggestion to SMS auth to payment, against stubbed Stripe and Twilio. It's also the date of pull request #1, the first time the project used a branch and review flow instead of me pushing straight to main, and the day a preview build taught me that environment variables don't copy themselves to new environments.

May 20 is the single biggest day in the repo: 78 commits. For a while I misremembered it as a blur, but the log narrates it fine. It's the day the mechanic's side of the product got built: a bay view with clock-in and clock-out, per-line job status, photo capture, tech notes, parts requests with an approval flow on the advisor's side, a sticky "clocked in" banner with a live timer. Same day: a shared list shell with search and pagination that four surfaces adopted at once, the SMS inbox with two-pane threads and a segment counter, and a sweep that killed every window.confirm() and window.prompt() in the app for real components. Seventy-eight commits sounds insane until you know it was three coordinated builds landing together.

The founding offer also has its birthday in May: a promo code called FOUNDER90 first appears in a commit on May 14, wired into checkout and mentioned in the mailer. File that away, because there's a confession about that code in the August section.

Deletions in May: the agency mode (a whole white-label concept, killed and repurposed into a founder-invite flow), the auto-seeded sample data for new shops (cold-start noise nobody asked for), and the nine-step product tour, replaced by three steps and a help center with ten guides and spotlight tours. Also the first two waves of an em-dash purge across the marketing site, which sounds cosmetic until you realize the goal was making every sentence on the site sound like a person.

What May taught me: what a unit test actually buys you, what end-to-end means, what a webhook signature verifies, why token hashing algorithm choice is an indexing decision, and that the mechanic and the service advisor are two different users who need two different products that share one database.

June: infrastructure month, or, everything that can silently fail will silently fail

June is when I stopped believing dashboards.

The Redis I used for rate limiting ran on a free tier that auto-deletes the database after 14 days of inactivity. Mine got reaped. Every Redis call then retried for about five seconds and failed open, which meant the lead-capture API took five seconds and rate limiting was silently off. In my head, "rate limiting: on" had been true for weeks. The fix was a one-second timeout that fails open fast, plus a daily keep-alive cron so the reaper can never fire again. Then, a week later, an audit found that the cron that delivers SMS campaigns had never been scheduled at all. The feature existed, the tests passed, the button worked, and no message would ever have sent. Two different features, same lesson: code that exists is not code that runs. Something has to arm it, and something has to prove it fired.

June 6 was the second row-level-security incident: eight tables added after the April audit had shipped without RLS, readable by anonymous clients, including payment-adjacent data. Fixed the same day I found it, along with standing up an admin logs console, TOTP two-factor on the admin account, and email alerting for errors and abuse spikes. I told myself I'd be more careful about RLS. Hold that thought until August.

Also June 6: I added bot protection as a hard block on the public forms. On June 7 I reversed it to log-only, because the bot checker flags anyone whose browser can't run its JavaScript challenge cleanly, and the number one place that happens is the Instagram in-app browser, which is exactly where a shop owner lands after tapping a link. I found out by getting blocked myself, on my own form, on a normal submit. Security that eats your funnel is friendly fire.

The good infrastructure of June: nightly encrypted database backups to Cloudflare R2, and, more importantly, an actual restore drill, because a backup you've never restored is a rumor. It passed, after teaching me a caveat about Postgres extensions on bare targets. A k6 load test rammed the public and authed surfaces at 3,000 requests per second to find the database tier's breaking point before mailing thousands of postcards at it; the verdict was go. Sentry went in alongside the homegrown alerting. An SSRF fix pinned outbound site-audit fetches to a validated IP, which is the day I learned what DNS rebinding is: a hostile domain can answer DNS with a public address once, pass your safety check, then answer with a private address when you actually connect, so you have to resolve once and connect to the literal IP you vetted.

June was also lead-machine month, because postcards were coming. A free website-audit tool: give it your shop's URL, it fetches the homepage (through that SSRF-hardened fetcher, with byte caps and a daily AI budget), grades it, and emails a scored report. A Google Business Profile grader with real, step-by-step fixes and the shop's actual review link. A declined-work calculator. A tools hub tying them together. An email nurture engine with scheduling, suppression, unsubscribe tokens, and attribution stamped at signup. And the scorecard funnel itself: an OutreachShop table, non-guessable tokens, a per-shop report page, and a QR pipeline that renders the shop's own numbers onto a 9x6 postcard with a logo-centered QR code and a spot-UV mask layer for the printer. Plus the boring parts that make mail real: suppression lists, a mail-house CSV export, pre-print QA with exclusions, and print-validation on every QR.

Product work kept moving under all that: opt-in two-factor for shop owners (authenticator first, then an email-code second factor that became the primary, with one-minute codes and rate-limited resends), Google sign-in dark-launched behind a flag, a rebuilt demo with a story-driven tour, a first-party analytics beacon, real unread tracking in the inbox, a practice floor for onboarding where a new owner writes a fake repair order for a fake customer named Maria Lopez and her 2018 Civic before touching real data, the email-confirmation wall deleted from signup, a VIN scan that reads the door-jamb barcode with the phone camera, and a dashboard redesign around one hero number: money collected this month. The end of June was a full homepage rebuild with a new type system, and an adversarial audit that found, among other things, that the contact CSV importer was rejecting 100 percent of rows because no consent value survived the mapping. One hundred percent. Tests green.

Deletions in June: the fake URL bars on demo screens, the "New" badges everywhere, the Pipeline/Deals surface (a CRM concept shops don't think in), the auto-firing welcome tour, and the email-confirmation wall. Every one of these was me admitting the app was cosplaying as enterprise software instead of being a tool for a person at a counter.

What June taught me: free tiers are landmines with timers, crons need proof of life, backups are rumors until restored, DNS rebinding exists, load testing is cheaper than an outage, and "the tests pass" and "it works" are different sentences.

July: the month everything got rebuilt, and the month I stopped trusting my own claims

July has too much in it. I'll take it in five swings.

The rebuild fortnight

July 1: a security audit found the one real injection bug of the project, in email rendering. The helper that wraps text into email paragraphs was interpolating raw strings, and one public route (online booking) let a stranger set their own first name, which crons would later render into shop-branded emails. The fix took the helper safe-by-default, added an explicit raw variant for developer-authored markup, and migrated about thirty call sites. July 2: the entire in-app product got rebuilt in one day, 48 commits on a branch: new design language, role system with owner, front desk, and mechanic roles, VIN scanning through AI vision with check-digit validation, rebuilt onboarding, 311 pages compiling. July 3, the morning after, I typed the most important message of the project: "i built this app and i dont understand how it works." So we simplified: navigation cut from 17 items to 9, estimates and repair orders and inspections collapsed into one Jobs list, the dashboard stripped from nine blocks to four, and a 17-feature learning hub I had shipped twenty-four hours earlier, deleted. Deleting a day-old feature stung. It was also obviously correct, and the discipline that made it safe was that the engine underneath, the schema, services, and APIs, was held frozen while the surface simplified.

July 4: the repair order became the command center, with the customer conversation, the inspection, and every verb living on the ticket. The mechanic door went in: tap your name on a shop tablet, get a scoped view with no money in it, locked with a per-tech PIN, and a kiosk mode that survives refreshes. Hours after shipping it, my own adversarial audit found the kiosk lockdown was bypassable, because the route guard lived inside a prefix check that three new routes didn't match. A locked tablet could type a URL and open the shop's financials. Fixed the same day, along with the fourth recurrence of the PATCH null-wipe bug, this time on inspections. Shipped, verified, and wrong within hours. That audit habit, attacking my own fresh work like a stranger would, is the single most useful engineering behavior I picked up all summer.

The robots

July 10, I stood up a second repo that is effectively a company org chart implemented in cron: scheduled agents that read production telemetry, write me a morning brief, hunt bugs at night, and open pull requests, with nothing merging until I tap approve on my phone, and the approval pinned to the exact commit SHA. Its first unsupervised night, it found the PATCH null-wipe bug live in production routes I hadn't audited, and PR #5 merged from my bed. Over the month it fixed STOP-keyword edge cases (a customer texting "STOP." with a period was not being unsubscribed, which is a compliance failure, not a typo), tightened tenant scoping, enforced two-factor on routes that had dodged it, and ran claims audits I'd never have the patience to run. It also once proposed a contact-deduplication change that would have merged 400 customers into one. I rejected that from my phone, then spent a weekend writing the identity-resolution module myself: 9,314 lines with tests. The system does a lot of my job now. That weekend it needed me to do mine. The whole thing, including its five-day silent notification outage and the morning it confidently cited a database column nothing ever writes, has its own write-up.

The truth sweep

Mid-July I pointed a fleet of review agents at one question: find every claim our marketing makes that the code doesn't back. 86 findings came back, 77 survived verification as genuinely false. The interesting decision was what to do. The cheap fix is softening copy. Mostly, I changed the product until the sentence became true: demo orgs really were sending emails (six had escaped to real addresses, now sealed), appointment reminders for business-hours appointments had never sent once (a cron audit found that; nobody noticed, including me), expired trials kept sending automated messages because nothing checked trial status, and the sentiment filter from April 13, the one that only asked happy customers for Google reviews, got killed entirely. That's called review gating, Google's policy prohibits it, and "we help your reviews" had been quietly resting on it for three months. Everyone gets the link now. Then the discipline became structural: a test fails the build if a branch touches product code without updating the changelog, and a family of tests pins marketing sentences to what the code actually does. There is deliberately no skip flag. I don't get to be trusted on this; the build does.

The wipe

July 23. Here's the message, verbatim, typos and all: "cleaning up all accouts on app starting from zero the shops that were testing for free never really utilized it deleted every single account." Free pilot shops from the early months had poked around and drifted off, and their rows made every dashboard number a small lie. So I deleted all of them and declared zero the baseline. The same day's audits made the wipe feel less noble and more overdue: every owner account created since the July 2 rebuild had been provisioned into the wrong role, locked out of reports and billing, meaning a trial that wanted to convert could not physically pay me. And repair orders had never charged the parts tax a shop configured. Weeks, live, in production. If anyone had converted in that window, my signup bug would have blocked the payment my tax bug was undercalculating. So the honest reading of July 23: what I deleted was the evidence that I'd never earned a customer base, and the same day I fixed two bugs that guaranteed I couldn't. Zero was the first true number that dashboard ever showed me. The same 48 hours, because momentum is a hell of a drug: a public help center with 79 articles, fourteen dead settings knobs wired to real behavior, account basics like sign-out-everywhere and 2FA device revocation, a WCAG AA accessibility pass with typed signatures on the money path, share cards on all 198 public URLs, and "Opening Day," a guided first-hour flow where a new shop's owner writes their first real repair order on their own data.

The applications and the ports

July 28, 2:24am, I submitted to Y Combinator. The written part got done; the phone video nearly didn't, and my whole prep for it is one message that says "man i dont know what to say." My private answer to "why are you doing this" was "im sick and tired of my job and want to make money," which is less poetic than what founders usually print and more true than most of it. The next evening I typed "nah nvm i dont think were getting in what about quality of life upgrades?" and went back to shipping. No response as of this writing, and both facts can sit there. The same week: a public changelog covering every ship day since April, a privacy-policy deepening called the Privacy Promise, a humanizer pass over all 54 blog posts, and the app's third design system. Yes, third. The July 2 rebuild's hand-rolled kit lasted 29 days before I ported everything onto a maintained open-source system, and on July 31 the old kit was deleted outright, in a merge literally titled "one design system, enforced by deletion." Keeping the old tokens around "just in case" is how you end up with two design systems and zero. And on July 31, after a printing saga I'll spare you (the vendor printed samples with the personalization missing; I caught a centering bug on one variant only because I ran my own physical scan test), 3,005 postcards went to USPS, each printed with that specific shop's Google rating, review count, and a modeled dollar figure, with a QR to that shop's own scorecard page. That campaign has its own write-up too.

What July taught me: role systems are provisioning systems (the P0 was born in a migration default), an audit the same day as the ship catches what the ship's own tests can't, marketing claims are code claims, and deletion is a feature with a changelog entry.

The A2P file, or: what it takes to legally send one text message

This deserves its own section because nothing else in the project had this shape: a months-long thread where the hard part was never the code.

If a business texts American customers from software, carriers require A2P 10DLC registration: a verified brand (the legal entity), a registered campaign (what you send, with sample messages), and a use case, filed through The Campaign Registry. Get it wrong and your messages silently stop delivering, or your whole number gets flagged. For a product whose spine is "text the customer an estimate, an authorization request, a payment link," this is existential plumbing.

The architecture went in on April 27, six waves in one day: every shop gets its own Twilio subaccount, its own provisioned number, its own brand and campaign registration, webhook routing by account SID, per-subaccount signature validation, and a status-polling cron, all surfaced in a settings page where a shop finishes its own registration. The next day I removed tax IDs from my own database and rate-limited the registration form, because collecting EINs is a liability I didn't want to hold. May 14, the campaign filing went in, with legal pages rewritten to carry the SMS program disclosure and consent language the registry expects, and a dedicated opt-in disclosure page followed on May 19 along with a pre-registration compliance attestation gate. Late May, consent became schema: a required enum on every contact, an assertion at nine send sites, consent captured in the contact form, the CSV importer, and the walk-up flow, and the removal of any shared-sender fallback. If a shop's own registration isn't active, that shop sends nothing. There is no house number to quietly borrow, which is the compliant answer and also means a registration problem stops that shop's texting completely.

The filing came back Verified: use case Mixed, five sample message types, all transactional and service-care. Trust score: 18 out of 100, which is what a brand-new LLC with no sending history gets, and which caps throughput. Then in July I audited the whole thread and found a four-way contradiction I had shipped entirely by myself: the pricing page sold "SMS marketing," the campaign filing described transactional and service-care messages, the public trust page said shops don't send promotional SMS through Pitlane, and a cron was sending a day-90 win-back text with a dollar-off offer in it, which any carrier reads as promotional. Four surfaces, four claims, and no two of them agreed. Also, it turns out a Verified campaign's filing fields are locked in the console. I verified that in the DOM, not by eye: every textarea renders disabled. Amending meant delete-and-re-register, new vetting, and a gap with no campaign, on an 18-trust-score brand.

So on July 31 I shipped the honest version instead. Marketing SMS got gated behind a single switch, off. The free-text campaign blast composer became email-only, because a textbox a shop can type anything into can never be squared with a fixed filing. The day-90 win-back text kept its care ("it's been a while since we've seen your Civic") and lost its offer; the discount moved to the win-back email, which carriers don't govern. Every public surface that sold promotional SMS stopped, and the booking page got a consent checkbox whose label is a single constant quoted verbatim in the filing text, with a test that fails if the page and the filing ever drift. My own note from that week: "i paused text campaigns because i dont want to be waiting another month for twillo a2p to be approved." The re-file with a marketing declaration is written and sitting in a doc, ready for the day there's revenue to justify it, filed from a clean sending history instead of a caught contradiction.

What the A2P file taught me is the biggest single lesson of the project: compliance is not paperwork on top of the product. It is product. The filing, the trust page, the pricing table, and the cron are one claim in four places, and they will drift apart unless something mechanical holds them together.

August: one price, honest instruments, and the first real test

August 1 and 2, the pricing collapsed. I asked "what happens if we only have 1 tier," and twenty minutes later: "okay go for it 200 even ... make sure gates are deleted as well." One plan, $200 a month, everything included. The sweep touched about twenty surfaces, and the entire gate layer, 33 route call sites, the limits table, the upgrade components, was deleted rather than bypassed. The only survivor is a fair-use cap on AI features. Same day, the third row-level-security incident: one last table had shipped without RLS. Third strike, so this time the fix came with a coverage test that enumerates every public table and fails the build if any future table ships without RLS. That's the pattern I trust now: the first incident gets a fix, the second gets a warning I'll ignore, the third gets a guard that doesn't need me.

August 3 was a strange, load-bearing day: I deleted the public roadmap entirely and demoted 133 of the changelog's 158 bullets into quiet "bug fixes and other improvements" counts. Publishing a ranked list of your own gaps reads as honesty from the inside and as a shopping list from outside. The rule that survived: the changelog never lies, and it also stops volunteering weaknesses, with a guard test enforcing both directions. Same day, the region moved next to the database in Ohio, which made database-backed pages roughly two and a half times faster, and the customer importer got rewritten around the identity module from the PR-#39 story, with a merge message I still like: "a shop's book arrives whole."

Then the postcards landed. August 4, first real scans. The first days of data showed a scan rate right at the honest ceiling for cold mail (that story, and the research pass behind it, is the postcard write-up), and showed the real leak: shops scanned, read, and left without giving an email. So the funnel got instruments: an event ledger from scan to landing to seeing the ask to typing to captured or failed or left, deduplicated, rate-limited on its own budget, carrying no personal data. The first three complete visitor stories it recorded told me more than four days of aggregate counts. And on August 5, the founding offer became real end to end: the first 25 shops get 30 days free, then three months at $0, then $200, enforced fail-closed in code. The confession attached to it: FOUNDER90, printed on all 3,005 postcards, had never existed in Stripe until August 2, two days after the cards mailed. Every card pointed at a checkout that would have said "invalid code." Caught it while rewiring checkout for one-tier, created it, and made the live offer strictly better than the printed one, so the gap between paper and product only ever runs in the customer's favor.

August 6, the night before I wrote this, I asked for research on the best-measured funnels, approved the plan with "yes go for everything im going to bed," and woke up to four merged branches: scan-to-signup attribution (before this, a shop that scanned and signed up directly was invisible), the scorecard page cut from thirteen exits to one, the email ladder grown from five sends to seven because the one peer-reviewed study on sequence length says under-mailing costs about twice what over-mailing does, and a day-3 trial email that offers twenty minutes with a person, which I have to personally honor. The suite ended the night at 5,037.

What August taught me: pricing is a hundred small claims pretending to be one number, guards beat vigilance, and the gap between what you printed and what you ship should only ever favor the customer.

What this was actually about

I restarted my laptop on August 6 and it told me the uptime: 101 days. It had been running continuously since the third week of the build. Every gate this project has passed, all five thousand tests, every typecheck, ran locally on that MacBook Air, because there is no CI server. The 203 merge commits each carry a one-line story ("a shop's book arrives whole," "the mechanic door holds," "the ask sits where the reader already is"), because I learned I need the history to read like a logbook when the memory of a week evaporates, and I have proof that it does: this entire timeline had to be reconstructed from session transcripts once, after I discovered a month of work had gone unrecorded.

If you're reading this as a hiring manager, here is the honest version of what I am. I can't whiteboard a red-black tree. I can take a product from an empty folder to production with payments, carrier-registered SMS, tenant isolation, backups with a passed restore drill, a 5,000-test suite, and a compliance file that matches the code, while holding a full-time job, and I can tell you the date and commit for every claim in this piece. The concepts I know, I know because they broke on me, in production, with my own name on the LLC.

Customer count today: still zero paying. The first scans are turning into captured emails, the founding offer is live end to end, and whoever converts first will be the first customer the product earned with true claims, working reminders, correct tax, and a payment page their role can actually reach.

Tomorrow is day 119.
